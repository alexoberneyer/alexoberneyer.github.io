01 / The ingredients

Change without a plan.

Imagine a population of green and brown beetles. Birds spot one color more easily in the current habitat. If the less visible beetles leave more surviving offspring, and offspring tend to inherit their parents’ color, the population can change across generations.

No beetle has to plan the change. The ingredients are variation, heritable differences, and unequal reproductive success.

Before trying the experiment: if green beetles leave 20% more surviving offspring, does a 20% starting frequency become 40% after one generation? Make a guess, or continue directly to the explanation.

01 / Variation

Individuals differ.

There is more than one variant for selection to act on.

02 / Inheritance

Some differences persist.

Offspring inherit something relevant to the difference in reproduction.

03 / Selection

Contributions differ.

Some heritable variants make a larger contribution to later generations.

Evolution is change in a population’s inherited characteristics across generations. Natural selection is one mechanism of that change. Individuals develop and learn during their lives; populations evolve across generations.

02 / Differential reproduction

Small advantages.
Many generations.

Start with 20 green and 80 brown beetles. In this toy model, a green beetle contributes an average of 1.2 surviving offspring for every 1 contributed by a brown beetle.

The expected next generation has 24 green and 80 brown offspring. Green’s share is 24 / 104 = 23.1%, not 40%. Reproductive contributions are multiplied first, then divided by the new total.

The beetle colors are an illustration: each variant is copied intact, as in a clonal or haploid model. This is not a model of real beetle mating, dominance, or diploid color genetics. It omits drift, mutation, migration, and changes in the environment.

pnext = p(1 + s) / [p(1 + s) + (1 − p)]p is green’s frequency, from 0 to 1. s is its reproductive advantage relative to brown; the slider uses −0.5 to +1. Green’s relative fitness is 1 + s; brown’s is 1. Frequencies have no units; a generation is one reproduction step.

At s = 0, the deterministic frequency stays unchanged. With a positive advantage, a present green variant increases; with a negative advantage, it decreases. If green starts absent, selection cannot create it. If brown starts absent, selection alone cannot bring it back. Displayed frequencies are rounded: 100.0% can mean nearly all green, not exact fixation.

How does this connect to allele frequencies?

An allele is a version of a gene. The recurrence also describes two alleles in a simple haploid population with fixed reproductive contributions. In a diploid sexual population, genotype fitness, dominance, mating, and inheritance have to be modeled explicitly.

With fixed fitnesses, the odds p/(1 − p) are multiplied by 1 + s each generation. That is the same normalization pattern you met in Bayesian updating, but reproductive success and evidence are different mechanisms.

03 / Context & mechanisms

Fit for what—and where?

In evolutionary biology, fitness concerns reproductive contribution in a particular context. It does not mean strongest, healthiest-looking, most complex, or morally best.

The insulating traits of a polar bear and the heat-shedding traits of a fennec fox illustrate why “better” needs an environment. A trait useful in one setting can be costly in another; a comparison across two species is not a fitness estimate.

Selection also has no foresight. It filters available variation under present conditions. Complex adaptations can accumulate through many inherited changes, but history, tradeoffs, and accessible intermediate steps constrain the routes.

ProcessWhat changesDoes it guarantee adaptation?
Natural selectionHeritable variants contribute unequally to reproduction.Can produce adaptation to the current environment; no universal optimum is guaranteed.
Genetic driftChance sampling changes frequencies in finite populations.No. A beneficial variant can be lost.
MutationNew genetic variants arise.No. Mutations do not generally appear because a future need requires them.
RecombinationExisting variants are rearranged into new combinations.No. It can change combinations without changing each allele’s frequency by itself.
Gene flowMigration and reproduction move variants between populations.No. Its effect depends on what arrives and where.

A frequency change alone does not demonstrate selection. To infer the mechanism, ask about sampling, migration, inheritance, and reproductive differences. The toy experiment isolates one cause; real populations combine several.

04 / Common ancestry

A branching history.

Populations can diverge after splitting, especially when gene exchange becomes restricted. Over time, barriers to interbreeding may develop: one route to speciation. Branching descent makes related groups share features inherited from common ancestors.

The tree of life represents relationships, not a ladder toward humans. Living bacteria are not unfinished animals. A lineage can simplify, diversify, or persist without becoming “more advanced.” Horizontal gene transfer also makes some evolutionary histories more network-like than a single clean tree.

05 / Memetic evolution

Ideas have transmission histories, too.

A tune, tool-making technique, slogan, or story can also vary, be transmitted, and become more or less common. Richard Dawkins introduced meme in The Selfish Gene (1976) for a cultural item viewed through this transmission lens. An internet meme is one familiar case, not the whole idea.

Ask which versions people remember, repeat, teach, or modify. A useful practice may spread because it works. A joke may spread because it is memorable. A false claim can spread because it is engaging. None of these routes makes popularity a test of truth.

After one round without checking, the counts are 120, 160, 110, and 140: B has 160 / 530 = 30.2%. With checking, B contributes 80 and has 80 / 450 = 17.8%. The change comes from the copying rule, not from a truth variable hidden in the equation.

Changing the checkbox recomputes the scenario with that rule applied from round zero. It does not switch policy halfway through a saved history.

This is an aggregate copying model, not a simulated social network. It has no limited attention, overlapping audiences, forgetting beyond round replacement, individual persuasion, or invented new ideas. Actual diffusion needs those mechanisms and evidence about them.

06 / The analogy’s boundary

People copy. They also reconstruct.

The analogy is useful, but cultural transmission is not literal gene copying. People can learn from peers, teachers, and media; combine traditions; reinterpret what they hear; or deliberately invent a new version. The “unit” being transmitted may have fuzzy boundaries.

QuestionGenetic inheritanceCultural transmission
What is passed on?DNA variants, in a biological inheritance system.Skills, practices, stories, representations; units depend on the question.
Along which routes?Parent–offspring transmission, with other routes such as horizontal transfer in some organisms.Across generations and among contemporaries; often many sources at once.
How does variation arise?Mutation and recombination, among other genetic processes.Copying differences, reconstruction, recombination, and deliberate innovation.
What counts as success?Contribution to later inherited populations.Depends on what you measure: retention, repeated use, adoption, or copies.

“Memetics” is a helpful framing, not a complete quantitative theory of culture. Cultural evolution research studies transmission and change without requiring every tradition to behave like a discrete gene. Experiments can distinguish copying from reconstruction, rather than assuming that similar outputs prove exact copying.

For software, scientific methods, or institutional habits, identify what persists, what changes, and how versions are evaluated. Deliberate design and reasoning remain part of the explanation; the evolutionary analogy does not make people passive copying machines.

07 / Gene–culture coevolution

Culture can change the environment
that selects genes.

Biological and cultural change can also influence one another. Dairying changes the food environment; genetic variants that maintain lactase production into adulthood can change the consequences of drinking lactose-rich milk.

01 / Cultural practice

Dairying and milk use

A learned practice changes which foods are available and consumed.

02 / Changed environment

Different costs and benefits

Digesting milk can matter differently under different diets and conditions.

03 / Genetic response

Selection on existing variants

Heritable differences can affect reproductive success over generations.

04 / Feedback to practice

Changed opportunities

Biology can alter the costs and benefits of continuing a cultural practice.

The fourth step can feed back into the first. This is a conceptual feedback structure, not a fitted historical model or a claim that every arrow was equally important everywhere.

The history is more complicated than “people drank milk, so they evolved to digest it.” Dairying preceded widespread lactase persistence in Europe. A 2022 analysis found that the intensity of milk use alone did not explain the inferred selection pattern; the authors proposed famine and pathogen exposure as contributing conditions. Their proposed drivers are hypotheses supported by model comparisons, not a settled account of every population.

Drinking milk does not train a needed inherited mutation into existence. Cultural practices change selection pressures on heritable variation. This is gene–culture coevolution.

08 / Retrieve & transfer

What is being passed on?

Keep one idea

Variation → inheritance → unequal persistence or reproduction → changed frequencies. Always ask what is copied, what the environment favors, and what the model leaves out.

Try explaining the mechanism without the page. Return another day and answer a transfer question before opening its feedback.

1. Recognition: does a beetle turning greener during its life prove evolution?

No. That may be development or an environmental response. To establish population evolution, look for a change in inherited characteristics across generations.

Look for: the distinction between an individual changing and inherited population composition changing.

2. Explanation: why does a 20% reproductive advantage not add 20 percentage points?

It multiplies green’s contribution. Starting with 20 green and 80 brown gives 24 green and 80 brown expected offspring, so green is 24/104 = 23.1%.

Look for: the new denominator, not just the 24 green offspring.

3. Transfer: a beneficial variant disappears from an island of ten beetles. Is selection disproved?

No. Chance in survival and reproduction can eliminate a beneficial variant in a small population. The deterministic model omits genetic drift. Other possibilities, such as a changed environment, also need checking.

Look for: an omitted process and evidence that could distinguish it from alternatives.

4. Transfer: a story is repeated a million times. What does that establish about its truth?

Repetition establishes spread, not truth. A shared original source can generate many dependent copies. Evaluate independent evidence and the transmission process.

Look for: a separation of copying success, evidential support, and usefulness.

5. Transfer: can one copying multiplier predict a rumor after everyone has heard it?

Not reliably. The toy model has unlimited opportunities for new active copies. Saturated audiences, repeated exposure, network structure, and changing interest break the fixed-multiplier assumption.

Look for: the failed assumption before extrapolating the curve.

Continue the connections: evidence and dependent reports, information and meaning, frequency-dependent strategies, and feedback and stability.
